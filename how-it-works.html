How This Trading Desk Works — Explained Simply

No finance background needed · plain English, everyday comparisons
← Back to daily briefings
TL;DR: Every morning the system shortlists ~25 good-quality stocks out of ~120 candidates, scores each one on three simple questions, and writes up a suggestion card. It never buys or sells anything by itself — a human always has to say "yes, go ahead" first.

The Big Picture

Think of this system as a very fast, very disciplined research assistant. Every trading day it:

  1. Shortlists stocks worth watching — like a scout filtering 120 job applicants down to 25 worth interviewing.
  2. Scores each one on three simple questions — is it going up? is it speeding up or slowing down? does the overall market feel good or bad today?
  3. Writes suggestion cards — "Consider buying AAPL, here's why, here's how much, here's when to bail out."
  4. Sometimes suggests an options trade — a higher-risk, higher-reward side bet, only on unusually strong days.

The assistant never signs the check. Every suggestion sits and waits for a human to approve it.

Part 1 — How Stocks Are Picked

Too Many Candidates to Check by Hand

Each morning there are roughly 120 stocks worth a look (from Robinhood's "Most Popular" and "Daily Movers" lists). Checking all 120 in detail would be slow and mostly wasted effort — most will get rejected anyway. So the system runs four quick, cheap checks first, like a bouncer checking IDs before anyone gets a full interview inside.

Filter 1 — Price must be above $15

Very cheap "penny stocks" often have a wide gap between the buy price and the sell price, quietly eating your money the moment you trade — even before the stock moves at all.

Everyday comparison: a currency booth that buys dollars at 95¢ and sells at $1.05 — you lose money just walking in and out. Cheap stocks behave the same way.

Filter 2 — Must trade at least $100 million a day

We only want stocks a lot of people are actively trading, so we can get in and out without our own order moving the price.

Everyday comparison: selling one raffle ticket in a stadium of 50,000 doesn't move ticket prices. Selling one ticket when only 10 people are in the room might. We stick to the "stadium" stocks.

Filter 3 — Be careful with big movers

A stock that dropped but is still fundamentally healthy might be a bargain. A stock that jumped more than 15% in a single day has probably already had its easy money made — buying now often means buying at the top.

Everyday comparison: a flash sale crowd rushing a store the moment doors open — by the time you hear about it, the good deals are already gone.

Filter 4 — Rank what's left, keep the top 25

Everything that survives Filters 1–3 gets a "quality score":

Quality Score = (How much money trades daily) × (How much it swings in price)
                ÷ (How wild it is vs. the overall market)
Everyday comparison: ranking used cars by (mileage they can still cover) × (how fun they are to drive) ÷ (how likely they are to break down). We want stocks that are active and interesting, but not reckless.

Only the top 25 make the daily watchlist.

Your Current Holdings Always Get Checked

If you already own a stock, it's automatically added to today's list — even if it fails every filter above. The question for something you already own isn't "should I buy this?", it's "should I sell, trim, or hold?" — and that needs answering every day, filters or not.

Part 2 — How a Stock Gets Its Score

Every stock on the watchlist gets asked three yes/no-ish questions, each worth -2 to +2 points:

Question 1 — "Trend": Is it going up?

Checks whether the recent average price is above the longer-term average, and whether that gap is widening.

Everyday comparison: is a runner's pace over the last mile faster than their pace over the last 10 miles, and is the gap growing? That's an accelerating uptrend.

Question 2 — "Momentum": Speeding up or running out of steam?

Standard trading gauges (RSI, MACD, TRIX — just names for dashboard dials) measure whether a price move still has energy left, or is stalling.

Everyday comparison: a speedometer tells you how fast you're going right now; momentum tools tell you whether you're still accelerating or coasting toward a stop.

Question 3 — "Macro Sentiment": Does the whole market feel good or bad today?

Broad market mood: how nervous investors are (a "fear gauge" called VIX), whether most stocks are up or down today, and a few other economy-wide signals — pulled from one trusted news source, never from betting markets strangers could manipulate.

Everyday comparison: even a great restaurant does badly on a night the whole town loses power. This checks whether "the whole town" (the market) is having a good day.

A Special Recession Warning: the "Un-Inverting" Yield Curve

One market-mood signal gets its own brake. Normally, lending to the government for 10 years pays more than for 2 years. Once in a while that flips (an "inverted" curve). The genuinely dangerous moment isn't the flip — it's when the curve flips back to normal after a long inversion, which has come right before most modern recessions.

Everyday comparison: it's like a fever that finally breaks. You'd think that means you're better — but for this particular illness, the temperature dropping is the exact moment the serious phase begins.

So when the curve un-inverts after a long stretch upside-down, the system holds the market-mood score down (never better than slightly negative) for about six months, however sunny everything else looks. A caution flag, not a prediction.

Adding It Up: -6 to +6

Total ScoreWhat it means
+6All three questions say yes strongly — everything lines up.
+4 to +5Most signs point up — a solid setup.
~0Mixed signals — no clear read.
-4 or lowerMost signs point down — avoid or consider selling.

The Score Isn't the Whole Story

A high score alone doesn't trigger a suggestion. The system also watches for patterns of change:

  • Running out of gas: great run, now showing tiredness → consider selling
  • Turning sour: score was positive, just flipped negative → consider selling
  • Bouncing back: score was very negative, now recovering → consider buying

How Much Money Goes Into One Trade

Simple rule: never risk more than 1.5% of your account on any single trade.

Everyday comparison: if you have $100 for a whole night at a casino, you don't put $50 on one hand of blackjack. Size each bet so one bad outcome doesn't wreck your night.

Worked example: $100,000 account → max $1,500 at risk per trade. Buying at $100 with a $95 bail-out price (risking $5/share) ≈ 300 shares.

The Total Risk Ceiling ("Portfolio Heat")

Beyond single trades, total risk across everything owned at once is capped — currently at 12% of the account.

Everyday comparison: one bad bet is annoying. Ten bad bets at once, because you were "all in" everywhere, is how people actually go broke. This cap prevents that pile-up.

Part 4 — When Options Are Suggested

What's an Option, in Plain English?

Buying a stock means owning a slice of a company. Buying a call option instead means buying a coupon that lets you purchase the stock at a fixed price later, for a much smaller upfront cost — but the coupon expires, and if the stock doesn't move enough in time, it becomes worthless.

Everyday comparison: paying a small deposit today to lock in today's price on a big purchase, with the right (not obligation) to buy later. If the price surges before the deposit expires, you win big for a small upfront cost. If it doesn't move, you lose only the deposit.

When Do We Suggest One?

Only on unusually strong days — all three scoring questions firing at once (total score +4 or higher) and spare money left in the day's budget after the regular stock suggestions.

What Kind, and How We Manage It

  • Only a call option betting the stock goes up
  • Roughly 5–8 weeks to expiry — enough time to play out, not so much you're paying for time you don't need
  • Needs a real price move to pay off, not a 1% wiggle — no betting on noise
  • Only options that are easy to get in and out of
  • Among qualifiers, the one most sensitive to the stock's price (biggest bang for the buck if right)

Exit rules: take the win at +100% value, cut the loss at -50%, get out if the strong-day signal disappears, get out within about a week of expiry.

Important: You Place These Yourself

The system never buys or sells an options contract. It proposes the idea and shows the math (worst-case loss, break-even point, rough odds of success). Like it → place it yourself in your own account. Don't like it → skip it, no harm done.

Part 5 — The Safety Rails

Even with a strong signal, the system runs on brakes, not just an accelerator:

  1. Per-trade cap: never more than 1.5% of the account at risk on one idea
  2. Total risk cap: never more than 12% of the account at risk across everything at once
  3. "Bad day" shutoff: unusually scary market or a rough account stretch → stop suggesting new buys, just watch
  4. Automatic bail-out price on every trade, agreed upfront rather than hoped for later
  5. No industry pile-up: limits on how much can concentrate in a single sector at once
  6. Buy smaller when the weather turns: on top of the fear-gauge dial-down, warning signs in the broad economy (high inflation, a contracting economy, or the recession-warning yield curve above) automatically shrink every new buy — to three-quarters, or half in the worst case. These stack: a scary and recession-warning day can cut new buys to a quarter of normal. Unspent money just waits for a calmer day.
  7. Lean toward defensive industries in a downturn: during those same stretches the system slightly favors things people buy no matter what (energy, household staples) and backs away from "nice to have" spending (retail, travel, luxury). It never forces a trade — it just re-orders the priorities.
Everyday comparison: you still drive when it's raining, but you slow down and leave more room; heavy rain and fog means you crawl. And when money's tight, people still pay for gas and groceries but skip the fancy dinner — the system tilts the same way.

Positions That Are Off-Limits for Selling

Some holdings are marked "protected" — long-term positions kept no matter what. The system tracks them for information only and will never suggest selling them, no matter how the score looks.

Part 6 — The Whole Day in One Paragraph

Every morning: shortlist ~120 candidates down to ~25 (plus anything already owned), score each one on trend / momentum / market mood, look for patterns of change, write suggestion cards for anything actionable, occasionally add an options idea on an unusually strong day, and save everything to a private log. Nothing gets bought or sold until a human reads the card and says yes.

Why cards instead of full autopilot? Finding a good opportunity is most of the work — but knowing when to walk away from a mediocre one, and staying calm when something unexpected happens, is a human judgment call. This system does the finding; a human keeps the final say.
